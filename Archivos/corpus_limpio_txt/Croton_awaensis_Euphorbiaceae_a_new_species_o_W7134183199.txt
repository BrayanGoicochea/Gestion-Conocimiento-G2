Vol.:(0123456789) Plant Systematics and Evolution (2026) 312:13 https://doi.org/10.1007/s00606-026-01984-3 RESEARCH Croton awaensis (Euphorbiaceae), a new species of dragon’s blood tree from the Chocó‑Andean region Michelle Suntaxi‑Oña1 · Catalina Quintana1 · Daniel Bellido‑Field2 · José Murillo‑A3 · Ricarda Riina4 Received: 5 September 2025 / Accepted: 11 January 2026 / Published online: 8 March 2026 © The Author(s) 2026 Abstract We describe and illustrate Croton awaensis, a new species of dragon’s blood tree (Croton sect. Cyclostigma) with medicinal value distributed in the Chocó and Andean regions of Ecuador and Colombia. The species is a medium-sized tree occurring in humid lowland and lower montane forests, between 300 to 1500 m. It is characterized by a sublepidote indumentum, falciform stipules, 2 acropetiolar nectaries on the abaxial side of the petiole and 0–8 on the adaxial side, shortly pedicellate fruits, and bifid styles. Croton awaensis could be potentially confused with C. floccosus or C. coriaceus, which also grow in the northern Andean humid montane forests of Ecuador but at higher elevations. The new species grows sympatrically, sharing the same habitat and location, with C. chocoanus, another tree species that belongs to a different section in the genus (C. sect. Luntia). Phylogenetic analysis of DNA sequence data from the nuclear ribosomal ITS region supports the placement of the C. awaensis in C. sect. Cyclostigma and its closest relationship with a clade including several dragon’s blood species from northern South America and Mesoamerica. We present and illustrate a set of morphological characters to aid in the identification of specimens of C. awaensis using images from herbarium material and field observations. A preliminary conservation assessment indicates that the new species is likely endangered due to habitat loss resulting from agricultural expansion and the scarcity of protected areas within its distribution range. Cyclostigma · Neotropics · Taxonomy Introduction While working on an inventory of Croton trees from Andean region, we came across several collections from Ecuador and Colombia that had been previously misidentified or included as indeterminate material in herbaria. A preliminary analysis of the morphology of these specimens allowed us to hypothesize that they likely belonged to an undescribed species of Croton sect. Cyclostigma. The majority of the Andean Croton trees belong to this section, which is among the most species-rich Neotropical clades in the genus (Riina et al. 2009; Van Ee et al. 2011). Members of sect. Cyclostigma are commonly known as dragon’s blood trees (“sangre de drago” or “sangre de grado” in Spanish) due to the presence of copious red latex, which is widely used for medicinal purposes in several Latin American countries (Meza 1999; Jones 2003; King et al. 2020). Croton sect. Cyclostigma as defined in the current classification of the genus (Van Ee et al. 2011) comprises ca. 50 species of small- and medium-sized trees. Although the section has a wide geographic range, stretching from Mexico to northern Argentina, most of its diversity is concentrated in the montane forests along the Andes with ca. 32 species. The second center of diversity is located in the Brazilian Atlantic forest, which is home to approximately ten species (Santos et al. 2017; Farias et al. 2019). In this paper we name, describe, and illustrate a new species of the dragon's blood clade of Croton (C. sect. Cyclostigma). We compare the morphology and distribution of C. awaensis sp. nov. with those of C. floccosus and C. coriaceus, the two morphologically most similar species within section Cyclostigma. The new species is also placed in a phylogenetic context based on sequence data from the ITS region (nuclear ribosomal Internal Transcribed Spacers) to confirm its placement in sect. Cyclostigma and to infer its phylogenetic relationships. Finally, we provide a distribution map and a preliminary assessment of the species conservation status. Materials and methods Phylogenetic analysis We used the ITS region, which has been employed extensively in numerous studies of Croton phylogenetics (e.g., Berry et al. 2005; van Ee & Berry 2011, 2021; van Ee et al. 2011; Haber et al. 2017; Riina et al. 2021; Chaves-Beyoda 2022; Rossine et al. 2025), as a DNA-barcode with reasonable results despite its limitations (Feliner & Rosello, 2007). Taxon sampling for phylogenetic analysis included 37 accessions corresponding to 34 species of Croton and one outgroup (Brasiliocroton mamonhinha), which is wellestablished as the sister-group to the entire genus (Berry et al. 2005; Riina et al. 2014). Of these, 19 sequences belong to the focal group (C. sect. Cyclostigma) including two newly generated sequences of C. awaensis, representing its distribution in Ecuador and Colombia. Most of the sequences used were from previous phylogenetic analyses at the genus level (van Ee et al. 2011) or from studies with a particular focus on sect. Cyclostigma (Riina et al. 2009; Mateo-Ramírez & Riina 2020), and were downloaded from GenBank (https://​www.​ncbi.​nlm.​nih.​gov/​genba​nk/). See Table 1 for GenBank accession numbers and voucher information. The DNA extraction of two specimens of C. awaensis, amplification, sequencing, and alignment followed the same laboratory and bioinformatics procedures used in Rossine et al. (2025). The aligned ITS matrix is provided as a fasta file (Online Resource 1). The phylogenetic analysis was performed in MrBayes ver. 3.2.7 (Ronquist et al. 2012) applying the GTR + I + G model following Masa-Iranzo et al. (2021). We did 2 runs for 10 million generations sampling every 1000th generation, and assessed the standard deviation of split frequencies for convergence (< 0.01). Tracer ver. 1.7.2 (Rambaut et al. 2014) was used to evaluate the Markov chains. The first 25% of MCMC samples were discarded as burn-in. Using the sumt command we obtained a Bayesian tree with posterior probability (PP) values by computing a majority rule consensus of the post-burn-in trees combined from both runs. We used FigTree ver. 1.4.4 (Rambaut 2006) to visualize and edit the consensus tree. We also performed a Maximum Likelihood analysis in IQTREE version 1.6.12 (Lam-Tung et al. 2015), using the same model as in the Bayesian analysis. Morphological analysis and taxonomy We examined collections of the putative new species and compared them with similar species of Croton. From this initial assessment the morphological comparison was narrowed down to the most similar species in C. sect. Cyclostigma (C. coriaceus, C. floccosus), which also occur near the area of distribution of the new species. We studied specimens of these three species from the following herbaria: AAU, B, CHEP, COL, CUVC, DAV, ECUAMZ, F, G, GB, GH, HUTI, K, LE, LOJA, MA, MEXU, MICH, MO, NY, PE, PH, PSO, Q, QAP, QCA, QCNE, SIO, TEX, U, US, and WIS. The protologues as well as the type materials and additional specimens from morphologically similar species in C. sect. Cyclostigma were also examined (Online Resource 2). Acronyms of herbaria follow Index Herbariorum (https:// sweetgum.nybg.org/science/ih/). For morphological descriptions we used a stereomicroscope with 10 − 60 × magnification. The morphological descriptions follow recent taxonomic and morpho-anatomical studies in Croton (Vitarelli et al. 2015, 2021; Feio et al. 2018a, 2018b; Riina et al. 2021). For trichome terminology, we followed the most recent classification of Croton trichomes (PintoSilva et al. 2023). Compilation of data for conservation evaluation Field trips were carried out during 2025 to verify some of the known localities of the new species, search for new populations, and to observe specific characters in vivo. The information about habitat, geographic coordinates, and locality was taken from examined herbarium collections and direct field observations. We converted the sexagesimal coordinates included in herbarium labels to decimal format to allow for proper recognition and processing by spatial analysis tools such as ArcGIS, and GeoCAT. For those without coordinates, we estimated them with Google Earth using locality and habitat information provided in

Species conservation status evaluation Based on the GeoCAT analysis, we obtained an EOO of 6,266 ­km2, indicating that C. awaensis could be Vulnerable (VU), and an AOO of 64 ­km2, suggesting it should be classified as Endangered (EN), according to IUCN Criterion B. In addition, the new species was preliminary assessed as Endangered under Criterion B2 because it has a restricted Area of Occupancy (AOO) of 60 ­km2, which is below the threshold for Endangered (< 500 ­km2). Croton awaensis is known from a limited number of localities, and there is evidence of a continuing decline in the quality and extent of its natural habitat. These factors meet subcriterion (a)—restricted number of locations—and subcriterion (b)(iii)—continuing decline in habitat quality. Even if the Extent of Occurrence (EOO) meets the threshold for Vulnerable, the more severe AOObased category applies. Therefore, the conservation status of C. awaensis was provisionally assigned as EN B2ab(iii). Discussion Species discovery in tropical areas remains a work in progress for many plant groups, particularly in species-rich genera such as Croton. Documenting and describing new biodiversity in this genus are urgent tasks as many of the recently described species occur in highly threatened species-rich biomes such as the Andean forest (e.g., Feio et al. 2018a, b; Riina et al. 2021), or the Brazilian Atlantic rain forest (e.g., Caruzo et al. 2008; Farias et al. 2019) and the seasonally dry forest (e.g., Sodré & Silva 2022; Martín-Muñoz et al. 2024; Santos et al. 2024; ZapataCorrea et al. 2024). The Chocó-Darién-Western Ecuador region (Myers et al. 2000)—or the Pacific lowlands sensu Bernal et al. (2016)—is also a biodiversity hotspot where botanical knowledge is still largely incomplete. The discovery and description of C. awaensis contribute to our understanding of the flora of this important and threatened tropical bioregion. Most of the records of the C. awaensis are from Ecuador with only two collections known from Colombia (Fig. 4). The scarcity of records in Colombia is likely a result of the limited botanical explorations in this country owing to more than five decades of armed conflict (Álvarez 2001; Sierra et al. 2017). Future botanical sampling of the Chocó-Andean region of Colombia will probably find additional occurrences of C. awaensis on the Colombian side. From a biogeographical perspective, the new species is interesting because it is one of two species of sect. Cyclostigma reaching the lowland forests of the Chocó bioregion. The other species of sect. Cyclostigma occurring in lowland areas of the Chocó bioregion is the Ecuadorian endemic C. churutensis Riina & Cornejo. However, C. churutensis occurs in seasonally dry forest from sea level to 700 m of elevation and its geographic range is more than 200 km apart from that of C. awaensis since it occupies a different latitudinal zone near the coast of southwestern Ecuador (Riina et al. 2007). Our phylogenetic analysis shows that the topology of the sampled Croton taxa (Fig. 1) is congruent with previous phylogenetic studies in Croton (Berry et al. 2005; Van Ee et al. 2011). Likewise, the structure of sect. Cyclostigma clade is also consistent with analyses that included a much broader representation of this section (Riina et al. 2009; Mateo-Ramírez & Riina 2020). The ITS data corroborate our initial hypothesis (based on morphology) that the new species belongs to sect. Cyclostigma. However, similar to the phylogenetic pattern found in previous studies, ITS alone cannot fully resolve (i.e., with high support) species relationships within sect. Cyclostigma. Although the phylogenetic results do not show the two C. awaensis accessions as monophyletic, they do not contradict the idea that they belong to the same species. To increase resolution and provide a solid phylogenetic framework for sect. Cyclostigma and a better delimitation of C. awaensis, future research in this group should employ a multigene or a phylogenomic approach using a comprehensive sampling of the section and a large representation of the nuclear genome. While ITS data alone do not provide a strong base for the taxonomic delimitation of C. awaensis, several morphological characters, along with geographic distribution, are useful for distinguishing the new species from similar species in its section. The three species can be distinguished by a set of morphological characters including trichome type, stipule shape, position, number and shape of acropetiolar nectary glands, as well as pistillate flowers and fruits features (Table 2, Fig. 5). Additionally, the geographic distribution and habitat of C. awaensis are also informative for species identification, since there is no overlap with the distributions and habitats of C. floccosus and C. coriaceus (Table 2, Fig. 4). The only Croton species that occurs sympatrically with C. awaensis is C. chocoanus Croizat. They grow frequently next to each other in the Chocó forests of Ecuador (field observation) and the same probably happens in the Colombian side, where C. chocoanus also occurs. The two species are easy to tell apart—since they belong to two different sections in the genus. Croton chocoanus (sect. Luntia) can be distinguished by its lobed leaves, sparse laminar nectary glands on both sides of the leaf blade, and multifid styles (vs. entire leaves, absence of laminar nectaries, and bifid styles in C. awaensis). Some specimens of C. awaensis have been annotated in herbaria with the names C. lechleri Müll.Arg., C. mutisianus Kunth or C. sampatik Müll.Arg. The first two species belong to sect. Cyclostigma and are indeed superficially similar to C. awaensis. Croton lechleri is currently under taxonomic revision, since the use of the name in the scientific literature and on herbarium collections appears to involve several confused taxa. However, even in its broad sense (C. lechleri s.l.) it can be differentiated from C. awaensis by its stellate indumentum (sublepidote in C. awaensis), subulate to cuculiform stipules, and its distribution in the eastern Andes-Amazonian foothills. So far Croton mutisianus is known only from Colombia, where it is widespread across high Andean humid montane forests between (1400)1800–2800 m; Murillo (1999) indicated the upper limit at 2400 m but our recent revisions of herbarium material shows that it can reach up to 2800 m. Besides geography and habitat, C. mutisianus can be differentiated from C. awaensis by its stellate, multiradiate, and dendritic trichomes, as well as the presence of more conspicuous and variable stipules, which go from subulate to slightly foliose with (sometimes) slightly dissected margins. The sequence of C. mutisianus included in our analysis (Fig. 1) shows that it is sister to the clade containing C. awaensis, however additional sampling with a better representation of the distribution and morphological variation of C. mutisianus is needed to confirm its taxonomic delimitation, phylogenetic position, and its relationship with C. awaensis. Finally, C. sampatik (sect. Sampatik) is different from C. awaensis in having abaxial, stipitate nectary glands on the lamina near the leaf margin, larger deeply lobed capsules (ca. 10 mm long), laterally compressed seeds, and a distribution in the AndesAmazonian foothills. Although our preliminary conservation assessment rendered Croton awaensis as Endangered, this result should be taken with caution. The species has a relatively wide geographic range along the western lower and middle slopes of the northern Andes of Colombia and Ecuador, but there are potential collection gaps in its distribution (Fig. 4). Most of the collection records are concentrated in the Esmeraldas and Carchi provinces in Ecuador. During our visit to the localities near the Lita–San Lorenzo road (Esmeraldas) in June 2025, we verified these occurrences and made new herbarium collections in nearby localities. We observed individuals of different ages (from juvenile to mature trees) suggesting that the species is regenerating. Specimens of C. awaensis have also been collected within the areas of Awá ethnic reserves in Ecuador and Colombia (Municipalities of Ricaurte and Barbacoas). Although these reserves are part of the national systems of protected areas (SNAP in Ecuador and SNIP in Colombia), they have been affected by mining and deforestation for agriculture and aquaculture. Outside of these reserves, a few collections have been made in Santo Domingo de los Tsáchilas Province in Ecuador. However, during our visit to these localities in June 2025, we were unable to find the species, as the forest vegetation has been largely converted into banana and cacao plantations. On the other hand, the closest SNAP protected area to the known distribution of C. awaensis is the Reserva Ecológica Cotacachi-Cayapas (RECC). It is possible that the species occurs in RECC since low montane forests are present within its limits (Elbers 2011). Other plant species with distributions and habitat preferences similar to those of C. awaensis are found within the limits of the RECC (e.g., Cornejo 2009; Orozco & Canal 2011; Cornejo & Mori 2012). Further botanical explorations targeting the gap areas within the current distribution range of the new species (Fig. 4) are necessary to better assess its conservation status. Etymology: The specific epithet is given in honor of the Awá indigenous people, who primarily inhabit the area of northern Ecuador and southern Colombia (Orejuela 1999). The Awás have traditionally used several Croton species for medicinal and ritual purposes. According to the labels of the herbarium specimens and one literature source (Ministerio de Educación del Ecuador 2021), Croton awaensis is used for eczema, wound healing and fungal infections. Description: Monoecious trees, up to 26 m high,10–40 cm dbh; young branches with a dense indumentum of sublepidote trichomes; ​mature branches glabrescent; latex red (main trunk and old branches) and yellowish (young branches). Stipules linear, caducous (only present on apical and young nodes), 4.9–5.2 × 0.8–1 mm, falciform, often embracing the stem, apex long-acuminate, margin with minute, scattered, globose glands (colleters), 0.1–0.2 mm long, glabrous on the abaxial side, with a dense indumentum of sublepidote trichomes on the adaxial side. Inflorescences terminal, erect, apical portion nodding, up to 35.5 cm long, axis costate, with a dense indumentum of sublepidote trichomes; bracts triangular, ca. 1 × 1 mm; cymules bisexual,each with 1 pistillate flower and 6–8 staminate flowers, toward the base of the inflorescence axis (staminate flowers at the base early caducous), regularly spaced at the lower section and more congested toward the apex. Staminate flowers pedicellate, pedicel 6–9 mm long, elongating with age; sepals 5, valvate, triangular-ovate, 2.5–3 × 0.9–1.3 mm, glabrous adaxial surface, abaxial surface with a dense indumentum of sublepidote trichomes; petals 5, narrow-elliptical, 2–2.4 × 0.9–1 mm, with scattered simple or stellate trichomes on abaxial side, margin lanate; receptacle densely pilose; stamens 15–16; filaments glabrous, 3–4 mm long, anthers 1–1.3 × 0.7–1 mm. Pistillate flowers pedicellate, pedicel 2.5–4.2 mm long, elongating with age, with sublepidote trichomes; sepals 5, valvate, triangular-ovate, 2.1–2.5 × 1.1–1.4 mm, glabrous on the adaxial side, abaxial side with a dense indumentum of sublepidote trichomes; petals absent or reduced to a filiform structure, 1.8–2.1 mm long; nectary disk segmented, segments opposite to the sepals, 1.8–2 mm long; ovary subglobose, with a dense indument of sublepidote, porrect, trichomes; styles bifid (six terminal branches), connate portion 0.9–1.1 mm long, free portion 2–2.4 mm long, glabrous, persistent. Capsules depressed subglobose, 4.1–5 × 4.5–5.3 mm, apical portion wider and slightly lobed; septicidally and loculicidally dehiscent; columella 3.3–4.5 mm long; seeds ovate, 3.5–4 × 2.9–3.1 mm, brown, coarsely rugose, shiny, caruncle triangular-trapezoid, 0.8–1 × 0.9–1 mm. Diagnosis: Croton awaensis belongs to C. sect. Cyclostigma, and within this section it is most similar to C. floccosus. It can be distinguished from the latter by the shorter pedicel of pistillate flowers (2.5–4.2 mm vs. 11–24 mm in C. floccosus), smaller pistillate sepals (2.1–2.5 × 1.1–1.4 mm vs. 4.1–4.7 × 1.8–2.7 mm), and smaller fruits (4.1–5 × 4.5–5.3 mm vs. 6–7.5 × 6.4–7.7 mm). Phenology: Data from herbarium specimens and our own field observations show that plants flower from April–June, and fruiting from November to February. Habitats: Primary and secondary Chocó lowland and Andean forests, in areas with high annual precipitation (4000–8000 mm). Distribution area: The new species occurs in northern Ecuador (Carchi, Esmeraldas and Santo Domingo de los Tsáchilas provinces) and southwestern Colombia (Department of Nariño), between (320–) 500–1500 m (Fig. 3; Online Resource 2).
